The Detective Mindset

When to use this: before any conversation with a business leader about learning.

About 2 min

Here's the uncomfortable part: most business leaders don't care how people learn. They care how people perform. When we talk in learning language, we sound like a cost center explaining its hobby. When we talk in results language, we sound like partners. Same work, different sentences, very different seat at the table.

First interviewThree rulesDictionarySo what?

The first interview

✕The order-taker trap

"My new managers are struggling. We need a leadership workshop ASAP."

The wrong reply asks about headcount and start dates, which locks you into a solution before anyone has named the problem.

✓The detective pivot

Say this instead:

I can help with that. Before we book anything, give me twenty minutes on what's happening on the ground. I want to know what this is costing us before I propose what fixes it.

Vendors take orders. Consultants ask what it costs.

Three rules for talking to executives

1

The Filter.

Does this make money, save money, or protect money? If it fits none of those, the business won't value it, however good the design.

2

Ditch the education jargon.

Bloom's, scaffolding, andragogy: keep them in the lab. Say "phased support" instead of scaffolding, "real-world application" instead of andragogy.

Nobody has ever bought andragogy.

3

Use "we", not "you".

You"Your team needs to learn X" makes you a finger-pointer.
We"We need ticket-handling speed down so we don't lose our best customers" makes you a co-owner.
Bucket Sounds like
Make We're cutting ramp-up time to speed up the sales cycle.
Save We're reducing error rates to cut rework.
Protect We're closing the compliance gap before an auditor finds it.

The translation dictionary

Your translation layer. The test for every "Try saying" phrase: does it end in make, save or protect?

Instead of saying Try saying Why it works
Training needs analysis Performance gap analysis Where the business is bleeding
Learning objectives Performance outcomes What the team does differently on Monday
Upskilling / reskilling Building capacity Work the team couldn't take on yesterday
Soft skills The behaviors: giving feedback, running meetings, writing clearly Leaders buy behaviors, not categories
Seat time / hours Time to proficiency Every learning hour is a non-working hour
Updating content Keeping company knowledge current The right answer, first time
Compliance training Risk mitigation Insurance against lawsuits and fines
Satisfaction scores Behavioral adoption Are they using the tools, not did they like the snacks
Completion rates Operational readiness Ready to run the next launch
Gamification Practice with a scoreboard Names the practice. "Gamification" sounds like games to a CFO

A direction, not a script. Every row ends at their number.

The "So what?" reality check

  1. The clue"We want to launch a communication skills path."
  2. So what?"So project updates are clearer."
  3. So what?"So PMs stop spending half their day chasing status."
  4. The hook"So we free up 20% of PM time for billable client work."

Keep asking until you hit make, save or protect. Run whatever is top of your queue through it now. No hook in four steps is worth knowing before the meeting.

Try it nowTake the last request in your inbox. Fill the C row in one sentence with a number in it. If you can't, that is Monday's first question.

C

C — Clarify the business goal

Ask this
  • If you keep one questionWhat's happening in the business that made this urgent now?The why-now probe. Urgency names the real trigger, often the real sponsor.
  • Imagine it's 90 days from now and this worked perfectly. What are people doing differently on Monday morning, and which number moved?The miracle question, borrowed from solution-focused coaching. Pulls a picture from a vague ask.
  • What is this costing us today: time, money, risk, or customers?
  • Who's losing sleep over that metric?
  • What have you already tried?

Watch out"We need training" is a solution wearing a goal's coat. Don't argue with it, keep asking about the problem until the coat comes off. And some requests are about visibility, not performance. "What does your leadership need to see, and by when?" is a legitimate C question, and a fix that also gives the sponsor something to show wins twice.

L

L — Look at the environment

You leave with the gap classified. Six kinds, adapted from Gilbert. Usually two or three at once.

  • Skill
  • Knowledge
  • Process
  • Tools
  • Incentives
  • Expectations
Ask this
  • Could your best performer do this today? What do they do differently?The bright-spot contrast. If one person can, it isn't purely skills, and you've found your expert witness.
  • On a scale of 0 to 10, how well does the team do this now? What makes it a 4 and not a 2?The scaling question. The second half names what works, which is where the fix hides.
  • What do we already have on this: tickets, error logs, CRM notes, survey verbatims?The paper trail. Cheaper than an interview, and it is what the sponsor will believe.
  • If you keep one questionIf we trained everyone tomorrow and the training was perfect, what would still get in the way?The most useful question in this guide. It hands you the environment gaps on a plate.
  • Do people know this is expected of them? And what happens when someone doesn't do it?
The field testCould they do it if their livelihood depended on it? If yes, you're not looking at a skill gap. (Mager and Pipe's classic.)

✓Training fixes this

  • KnowledgeNobody has shown them how
  • SkillTheory known, never done under pressure
  • KnowledgeA new system, process or policy landing next month
  • KnowledgeA product launch where the story is new
  • KnowledgeCompliance, where the record of training is the requirement

✕Training does not fix this

  • ProcessA step broken upstream
  • ToolsSystems that fail at the moment of use
  • IncentivesPay or targets that reward the wrong thing, or no consequence either way
  • IncentivesWork nobody is held to
  • ExpectationsNobody has said it's expected of them
  • ExpectationsManagers who don't manage
  • Outside the sixToo few people for the volume
  • Outside the sixA role that doesn't fit the person in it
  • Outside the sixA culture where naming the problem costs more than living with it

If the cause is on the "does not fix" list, the best course you ever built won't move the number. Most of those causes belong to someone who can change them in an afternoon, which makes them your handover list (→ 07). Headcount, hiring and culture sit outside all six. Not yours either, and the hardest handover in this guide. For now this is a hypothesis. The verdict version, once you have evidence, is in File 07.

Sort the evidence

Eight things a stakeholder might tell you. Drag each quote into a box, or tap it and then tap the box. On a keyboard, focus a quote and press 1 or 2.

    1A course can fix this

    • Knowledge"Nobody has walked the new hires through the returns screen."
    • Skill"They know the escalation rule. They've just never done it with a customer shouting."
    • Knowledge"The new policy lands next month and nobody has seen it."

    2Hand it over

    • Tools"The tracker asks for fifteen fields, so people update it at sprint end."
    • Process"Discount approval takes five days and two signatures."
    • Incentives"Handle time is 40% of the scorecard."
    • Expectations"Nobody ever said the cascade was the manager's job."
    • Tools"The best analyst has a template. Everyone else rebuilds the report by hand."

    Three of eight belong to a course. That ratio is about right.

    Watch outDon't run this step from a meeting room. Leaders describe the workflow they designed. The floor runs the workflow that survived. (→ Fingerprint Dust)

    U

    U — Uncover the root cause

    Ask this
    • If you keep one questionTell me about the last time this went wrong. Walk me through it, step by step.The last-time question. One real episode beats ten opinions about "usually".
    • Say more about that.The probe and the pause. Then count three seconds of silence. The answer after the silence is the better one.
    • If that were the cause, what would we expect to see in the data?
    • Who would disagree with this explanation?Triangulation. Now go talk to that person.
    • Run the 5 Whys chain (→ File 04)

    Watch outThe first plausible cause, and especially the cause your sponsor prefers. A detective doesn't close the case on the suspect who confesses fastest.

    See cold case 02 →

    E

    E — Execute a targeted solution

    Ask this, of the sponsor and of yourself
    • If you keep one questionWhat's the smallest pilot that would prove, or kill, this idea in six weeks?
    • Who has to reinforce this on Monday morning, and do they know it yet?
    • What do we measure, from what baseline, and who owns that number?
    • At the gate: what result means scale, what means adjust, what means stop?
    • What part of this will training not fix, and who owns that part?Say it in the recommendation. An unnamed cause becomes your failure.
    Write the hypothesis before you build anything
    The templateIf we intervention for audience, then metric will move from baseline to target within time, measured by source.
    One filled inIf we pre-brief managers 48 hours before each announcement, then the share of teams hearing it from their own manager within a week moves from a third to 80% in one quarter, measured by a two-question pulse.

    Watch outScope creeping back toward "a full course". The targeted fix you designed in week one grows modules by week four.

    Close the case

    01What we found 02What we did 03What moved 04What we recommend next

    Ten minutes of work, after the solution ships. The difference between "the training team" and "the people who fixed our discount problem".

    Two sizes of investigation

    Size oneThe quick read, 20 minutes.

    The C questions plus one L question, inside the conversation you were already having. Covers most requests.

    Size twoThe full investigation, one to two weeks.

    Interviews, shadowing, a look at the data, a Case File. For a big audience, real budget, an executive sponsor, or a monthly number.

    Say which one you're doing. The quick read is a gate, not a verdict. Upgrade it the moment it turns up something expensive.

    When they push back

    Sooner or later: "I don't have time for questions. Just build it." If you keep one line from this table, keep the last one. The parallel track costs the sponsor nothing, and it is the move that works when they outrank you by three levels.

    They say You say Why it works
    "Just build it." Makes sense, you need this moving. What's driving the date? A deadline you understand you can design around
    "I don't have two days." Two ways to go. We build exactly what you asked for, ready in three weeks. Or I spend two days with the team first and we build something narrower that's more likely to move your number. Your call. People defend the plan they picked
    "That's your job, not mine." You own the date. I own whether this works. Give me two days and I'll own both. Ownership named plainly beats method defended at length
    "We'll see how it goes." Let's build it. Can we look at the number together in six weeks and decide if it needs a second round? Turns a delivery into a case with a follow-up
    "The date is fixed, full stop." Then we run a parallel track. Design starts today, and in the same week I get three short conversations with the people who do the work. You lose nothing and the build lands closer to the problem

    And if they still say build it? Build it well, and write the hypothesis down anyway. If the number doesn't move, you hold the opening of the next conversation. If it moves, you were wrong and learned something. Both beat being right in private.

    The Gadgets

    When to use this: the conversation is drifting toward opinions and you need it back on evidence.

    About 3 min

    Four tools. Each exists because a stakeholder conversation, left unattended, drifts toward the loudest opinion in the room.

    5 WhysString BoardFingerprint DustWitness Interview

    The 5 Whys Magnifying Glass

    use during U

    Reach for it when a symptom is on the table and everyone already "knows" the cause.

    Ask They answer
    The symptom We need communication training.
    Why is communication the problem? Status reports arrive late and leadership gets blindsided.
    Why do reports arrive late? PMs wait days for engineers' status data.
    Why do they wait? Engineers only update the tracker at sprint end.
    Why only at sprint end? The tracker demands 15 fields and duplicates the dev tool.
    Why the duplication? The two systems were never integrated after the reorg.
    Verdict: a tooling and process case. Training PMs to "communicate better" would have treated the witness statement, not the crime.

    Before you trust itWhen a why has two answers, follow both. If a why lands on a person, you asked the wrong why, so back up and ask what makes that mistake easy. Verify the final why against data: the 5 Whys finds a thread, not the whole truth.

    The String Board

    use during C and L

    Four people, one question each, before you open any case.

    The Sponsor
    buys the solution
    "What does success look like on your dashboard?"
    The KPI Owner
    bleeds from the problem
    "Which number keeps you up at night, and where is it today?"
    The Performer
    does the work
    "Walk me through where this gets hard."
    The Reinforcer
    their direct manager
    "What would you need to see to coach this every week?"

    Red flagIf you can't name the Reinforcer, the case isn't ready to open.

    The Fingerprint Dust

    use during L

    Leaders design training around the dream workflow. You dust for the one that actually runs.

    1. 1

      Shadow two or three performers for an hour each.

      Watch, don't demo.

    2. 2

      Log every workaround.

      A workaround is someone already solving a process failure, informally and invisibly.

    3. 3

      Put the official process doc beside what you observed.

      Mark every difference.

    4. 4

      Note what the top performer skips.

      What experts skip is usually what the process got wrong.

    If the environment is broken, training people on the perfect way just teaches them to be frustrated accurately.

    Dust in daylightSay what you're looking for and why, check that now is a good moment, and show people what you noticed before you show anyone else. "I'm looking at the process, not at you" usually covers it.

    The Witness Interview

    use during C and U

    The technique the rest of the toolkit depends on. Stakeholders are witnesses, not suspects. Ask for 30 minutes and say you're there to fix the process, not to grade anyone.

    The move Say this Not this
    Open, don't lead What's getting in the way? "Is it the new tool?" A leading question returns your hypothesis in their voice
    Ask for the last time Tell me about the last late report. "Are reports usually late?" That gets a mood, not a fact
    Listen for verbs "They stopped raising risks in standup" is evidence "The team is disengaged" is a verdict. Ask: "What did that look like?"
    The three-second pause After their answer, wait. Count it silently. What comes next is usually the real answer Filling the silence yourself. "So would you say it's the tool?" hands them your answer to agree with
    Mirror the last few words "…so the handover just doesn't happen." "Doesn't happen?" They keep going. Borrowed from hostage negotiators Paraphrasing at length. "So what you're saying is…" turns their story into yours, and they stop telling it

    The 7 Rules of Evidence

    When to use this: you're about to design, pitch or report on a solution and want it to hold up under cross-examination.

    About 2 min

    Seven rules that keep a recommendation standing when the room turns hostile. Borrowed from how product teams ship, in three habits.

    Habit oneProve it

    01

    Outcome over output.

    Celebrate "onboarding time down 18%", never "10 modules delivered". (→ the Filter, File 02.)

    Do this: write the outcome sentence before the kickoff.
    02

    Hypothesis-driven.

    Not "we need leadership training" but a testable bet: if we give managers structured feedback practice, escalation cases drop 25% within three months, measured in the case tracker.

    Do this: if you can't fill the template, you're not ready to build.
    03

    Data first.

    Capture the baseline before anything ships. Screenshot the dashboard. That picture is your before-and-after story. A true control group is usually a fantasy in L&D, so use the realistic substitutes:

    • 1a staggered rollout, pilot group against not-yet group
    • 2a before-and-after on a stable metric
    • 3a comparison against the trend line
    Do this: no baseline, no launch.

    Habit twoShip it small

    04

    Ship the smallest pilot.

    One that produces a verdict. Iterate on behavior, not on feedback forms.

    Do this: one cohort, six weeks, one metric.
    05

    Give every asset a sunset date.

    Treat learning like a product: discovery → validation → launch → growth → optimization → sunset. Yes, sunset. Undead courses cost credibility every day they stay open.

    Do this: every asset gets a review date and an owner at birth.

    Habit threeDesign the system, not the course

    06

    Design the reinforcement loop.

    Training alone rarely survives the workplace. The manager's role, the tool changes and the workflow integration are part of the solution, or the solution fails.

    Do this: name the Reinforcer in the plan (→ String Board). If that box is empty, stop.
    07

    User and buyer.

    The user is the employee, the buyer is the business leader. Delight learners and move no metric, you lose the budget. Move the metric and torture learners, you lose the users.

    Do this: one sentence of value for each, side by side (→ Case File §2).

    When to use this: you want to see the whole method run once, start to verdict.

    About 4 min

    The main case: the cascade that never landed

    A composite of cases I've worked. The numbers show the shape, not one company's record.

    Work it yourself first? The solved case stays folded until you're done. About 4 min.

    Step What it revealed What changed
    !The request Middle managers can't communicate. We need communication training for all people managers. Leadership arrived with the verdict already written Nothing yet
    CClarify The survey had just landed, "I understand where the company is going" at 41%, and the board asked why A behavior instead of an adjective: each team hears the update from their own manager within a week
    LLook One department scores 78%. Its director pre-briefs her managers with a one-pager. Everyone else gets 60 slides at the same moment as their teams, no talk track, nowhere to ask, and nobody had said cascading was their job Gap classified: process and expectations, plus a thin, real skill need
    UUncover Three managers on the June pivot all learned it in the all-hands with their teams. Two improvised in the hallway within the hour (honestly, I know as much as you do). Verbatims clustered on "my manager couldn't explain why" Two independent sources, one cause
    EExecute One BU, one quarterly cycle: a 48-hour pre-brief, a one-page talk track, a 48-hour Q&A channel, one 90-minute practice session Hypothesis signed: 80% of teams hold the conversation within a week, survey item +5 from 41%
    ✓Verdict 9 of 10 pilot teams held the conversation inside a week. The pulse item gained 6 points. The hallway questions stopped Make the pre-brief permanent

    The primary metric is a behavior you can count next month, not a survey you wait a year for. Closing the 37-point gap to the best department in one quarter is a fantasy, and a sponsor who has read this guide would call it one.

    Case closed
    Found Did Moved Next
    Briefed last and armed with nothing Four fixes, one of them training 9 of 10 teams, +6 points Make the pre-brief permanent

    Training was a quarter of the fix, and it worked because the other three-quarters shipped first.

    Cold case 07 · open for two quarters

    The case we lost

    Asked for
    Sales enablement for a line nobody was selling.
    Question
    "What happens to a rep's payout when they sell the new line instead of the old one?"
    Turned out
    IncentivesThe new line paid less commission. No course out-argues a paycheck.
    Fix
    Not ours to make. Comp wasn't on the table, the training shipped as requested, and the number stayed flat, as the written hypothesis predicted. Two quarters later, when someone asked why the line still wasn't moving, that hypothesis was the only document in the room with an answer. Comp changed, and the training started working.
    • The diagnosis existed on paper before launch
    • The prediction was specific enough to check
    • Nobody said I told you so

    Who actually makes these fixes

    Ops changed the approval rule. The support lead reweighted the scorecard. IT flipped a permission. L&D owned none of those fixes, and that's the job.

    Bring the evidence, not the verdict.

    "Here's what we saw in six tickets" beats "your scorecard is wrong."

    Name one owner, not a department.

    A fix assigned to Ops is a fix nobody makes (→ String Board).

    Ask for the smallest change that tests the diagnosis.

    One field. One line in a policy. One calendar default.

    Let them have the win.

    The scorecard was their call.

    When training is the answer

    You ran L. Now you have evidence, not a hunch. Build only if all three "build" conditions hold.

    ✓Build the course when

    • The bright-spot performer succeeds because she knows something the others don't
    • The environment would let everyone do the same
    • Nobody has tried teaching it properly

    ✕Don't when

    • This exact training has already run and the number didn't move
    • Everybody knows the process and nobody follows it
    • The managers who would reinforce it aren't reinforcing anything today

    When the build list lines up, build it, and pilot it small anyway. And when the answer is training, you stand in front of people who have watched you turn down work that wouldn't have helped.
